In my previous posts, I delved into the inner workings of our smart devices. Now, I want to focus on another fundamental concept: how these devices connect and communicate through a network.

Imagine a small town where each house is a device. If no roads connect the houses, sending messages is difficult, slow, and unreliable, requiring travel through fields or forests. To solve this, the town builds paths and roads, enabling people to easily exchange letters or packages by walking, biking, or driving. This system of interconnected homes and paths is much like a basic network.

In technology, a network links devices so they can exchange information, which is essentially [just 1s and 0s](https://squareone.amitgawande.com/p/binary). These connections can be physical, like cables, or invisible, like Wi-Fi signals. Regardless of the method, the purpose remains the same: to enable devices to communicate effectively with one another.

## How Data Finds Its Way

In our town, for mail to be delivered correctly, each house needs a unique address—a number and street name—so the mail carrier knows exactly which door to approach. Without this, letters would be undeliverable.

Digital networks operate on a similar principle. Each device connected to a network is assigned a unique identifier, *known as an IP address (Internet Protocol address)*. This system is vital for the orderly flow of information, preventing data packets from getting lost as they navigate the vast web of interconnected devices.

## Keeping a Big Town Running

As our town grows with more houses and roads, things get more complex. Roads and addresses alone aren't enough; guides or stations are needed to direct traffic and prevent messages from getting lost, much like traffic police or a central post office sorting mail.

In computer networks, routers and switches — [powered again by tiny transistors](https://amitgawande.com/blog/2025/transistors) — act as digital post offices or traffic managers. They examine destination IP addresses and guide data along the most efficient path. Routers choose the best route from available options, similar to a GPS. Switches manage traffic within a local area, ensuring data reaches the correct device and preventing congestion.

## From Your Home to the Globe

Networks vary significantly in scale, much like our town analogy can expand.

- **Local Area Network (LAN)**: If our town is just a few houses connected by short paths, it’s like a LAN. Your home network, linking your computer, phone, and smart TV to your Wi-Fi router, is a perfect example.
- **Wide Area Network (WAN)**: When towns connect to other towns, possibly across states, countries, or even oceans, it resembles a WAN. These networks span larger geographical areas and are often used to link different branches of a company or to provide internet access over a broad region.
- **The Internet:** When all the world's networks—countless LANs and WANs—connect, they form the Internet. It's the ultimate WAN, connecting billions of devices worldwide and *forming the backbone of our digital lives*.

---

These fundamental concepts power almost everything we do online, from sending emails and making video calls to browsing websites and checking the weather. Understanding networks is like understanding how a city works. Once you know the basic concept of networks, concepts like email, websites, and the internet become more straightforward to comprehend.
